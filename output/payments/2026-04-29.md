**Council advances PSD3 and PSR final compromise texts to Coreper**

*The EU's revised payments framework has cleared a key procedural hurdle, with final compromise texts now before member state ambassadors for endorsement.*

On April 23, the Council of the European Union issued an "I" Item Note forwarding the finalised compromise texts of PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation to Coreper — the committee of permanent representatives that precedes formal Council adoption. The texts follow the provisional political agreement reached between the Council and the European Parliament in November 2025, and their publication marks the first time the full negotiated wording has been available for scrutiny.

For payments firms and their advisers, the published texts are the most concrete basis yet for implementation planning. Law firms such as William Fry and Bird & Bird have begun publishing initial analyses. The formal adoption process — plenary votes in both institutions — is the remaining step before the transposition clock starts for PSD3 and the directly applicable PSR takes effect.

The significance is practical: firms can now map compliance programmes against settled text rather than leaked drafts or political summaries. Areas of particular attention include the expanded scope of open banking access rights, fraud liability rules and the new licensing framework for payment institutions.

**UK confirms it will fold the Payment Systems Regulator into the FCA**

*HM Treasury has published its consultation response confirming the merger, settling months of uncertainty about the future shape of UK payments oversight.*

The UK government has confirmed it will consolidate the Payment Systems Regulator into the Financial Conduct Authority, subject to primary legislation. The decision, published alongside a broader package of payments reforms, resolves a question that had been open since the consultation launched earlier this year. The response sets out core design decisions for the merged structure, though the legislative timeline remains subject to parliamentary scheduling.

The same package addresses several other significant issues: new FCA powers over the future development of open banking, provisions to regulate stablecoin-based payments, and exploratory work on how AI agents conducting payments on behalf of consumers should be governed. The government has also signalled intent to modernise the Electronic Money Regulations and the Payment Services Regulations into a single coherent framework.

For the industry, the practical question is sequencing. The PSR-FCA merger requires primary legislation, while other elements such as FCA rule-making on open banking could move faster. Firms currently supervised by the PSR should expect a transitional period, but the direction of travel is now settled.

**FCA sets out roadmap to deliver open finance by 2030**

*The UK regulator has outlined how it intends to extend data-sharing obligations beyond payments accounts to products such as mortgages, pensions and investments.*

The FCA has published a detailed account of how it plans to move open finance from concept to operational reality by 2030, building on its new powers under the Smart Data framework. A Freshfields analysis of the regulator's position notes that while open banking is limited to payments data, open finance would extend the same consent-based data-sharing model across a much broader product set, with SME lending flagged as an early priority.

The regulator's approach leans heavily on the infrastructure and lessons of open banking, but the expansion raises distinct challenges: product complexity, data standardisation across heterogeneous sectors, and the question of whether third-party providers built for payments can scale into pensions or insurance data. A Mondaq regulatory round-up covering the week of April 17 notes the FCA will prioritise exploring how open finance can serve SMEs, suggesting a pragmatic, use-case-led rollout rather than a big-bang mandate.

For firms already operating in open banking, the 2030 target provides a planning horizon. For those in adjacent sectors such as wealth management and insurance, it is an early signal that API-based data access obligations are heading their way.

*Editorial note: The FCA's open finance roadmap gives concrete shape to what has been a vague aspiration — the 2030 target date and SME-first prioritisation are new and actionable details.*

**Token.io to co-lead new German open banking chapter**

*The payments infrastructure firm will help establish ETPPA Germany, a national body aiming to unify third-party providers in one of Europe's largest open banking markets.*

Token.io has been named as a co-lead of ETPPA Germany, a new national chapter of the European Third Party Providers Association. The chapter aims to create a unified platform for third-party providers operating in Germany, a market widely regarded as one of Europe's most important for open banking but one where fragmentation among providers has been a persistent challenge.

Germany's open banking landscape is shaped by the dominance of its Sparkassen and cooperative bank networks, which together account for a large share of retail accounts but have historically adopted API standards at varying speeds. A coordinated TPP voice could accelerate standardisation and provide a more effective counterparty for bank-side discussions on API quality and access.

The move also reflects Token.io's broader positioning as an infrastructure layer for account-to-account payments across Europe. Co-leading a national industry body is a different kind of market entry to signing individual bank partnerships, and signals an intent to shape the regulatory and technical environment rather than simply operate within it.

*Editorial note: Germany's importance to European open banking is well established, but progress has been uneven — a formal TPP coordination body is a notable structural development.*

**Spare launches open-banking-powered cross-border payments in the GCC**

*The UAE-based firm claims the first international open finance payments solution using the country's Al Tareq scheme, as Gulf regulators push to operationalise their frameworks.*

Spare has announced what it describes as the first cross-border payments solution in the GCC built on open banking rails, using the UAE's Al Tareq national open banking scheme for payment initiation. The company has received in-principle approval from the Central Bank of the UAE to operate under the country's Open Finance Regulatory Framework.

The announcement matters less for the individual company than for what it reveals about the pace of open finance deployment in the Gulf. The UAE's regulatory framework has moved quickly from publication to operational use, and cross-border functionality — connecting Al Tareq to schemes in other GCC states — represents an ambitious extension of what most European markets have achieved with domestic account-to-account payments.

For European observers, the GCC's approach offers a reference point. Where European open banking developed primarily around domestic account access and only belatedly addressed payments initiation, the Gulf model appears to be building cross-border capability into the infrastructure from the outset.

*Editorial note: The GCC's rapid operationalisation of open finance frameworks provides useful comparative context for European professionals tracking global trends in the space.*
