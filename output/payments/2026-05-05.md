**Freshfields maps out PSD3 and PSR implementation priorities for payments firms**

*A detailed legal analysis from Freshfields Bruckhaus Deringer sets out the practical consequences of the EU's new payments framework, highlighting the merger of e-money and payment institution regimes and the shift to directly applicable rules on open banking.*

The briefing, published May 4, is among the first major law firm analyses to walk through the PSD3/PSR package provision by provision with an operational lens. Two structural changes deserve attention. First, PSD3 folds the E-Money Directive into the payment services licensing regime, creating a single authorisation framework. For firms currently holding separate e-money licences, this means re-licensing under new conditions – a non-trivial compliance exercise with transition timelines that are not yet fully defined.

Second, the Payment Services Regulation applies directly across all member states without national transposition. This is a deliberate departure from the directive model that produced uneven implementation of PSD2. For open banking, the PSR preserves the dedicated interface requirement for account-servicing payment service providers but tightens the obligations around access and performance. The Freshfields analysis notes that the framework expands the scope of accounts covered and introduces provisions aimed at reducing the friction third-party providers have experienced under PSD2's API regime.

For firms planning their compliance roadmaps, the key question remains timing. The legislative texts are agreed but implementation dates and the associated regulatory technical standards from the EBA will determine the practical deadlines. Firms operating across multiple member states stand to benefit from regulatory harmonisation but face a front-loaded compliance burden during the transition.

*Editorial note: This is the most substantive legal analysis of PSD3/PSR to surface this week and offers practical detail beyond what the Commission's own summaries provide – directly relevant to compliance and product teams across the industry.*

**ECB annual report confirms instant payments regulation drove sharp uptake in 2025**

*The European Central Bank's 2025 annual report documents the effect of the Instant Payments Regulation on euro area adoption, providing the first official backward look at the regulation's initial impact.*

The ECB's report, published May 4, confirms that the requirement for euro area payment service providers to offer both send and receive capability for instant credit transfers produced a measurable shift in volumes during 2025. While the report does not break out granular transaction figures in the summary, it frames the regulation as a catalyst for infrastructure readiness across member states that had previously lagged.

This matters as a baseline for the policy debate now under way. With the regulation's receive mandate already in force and the send obligation having taken effect, the ECB's assessment provides the first institutional validation that mandating instant payments moves the needle. For payment processors and banks still investing in back-end upgrades, the report reinforces that the direction of travel is settled – the remaining question is how quickly instant payments become the default rail for retail and corporate transactions in the euro area, rather than a premium option.

*Editorial note: The ECB's annual report is a primary source confirming the practical effect of the Instant Payments Regulation – important evidence for infrastructure investment decisions and for the broader debate about mandated versus market-led adoption.*

*Sources: [Annual Report 2025](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/annual-reports-financial-statements/annual/html/ecb.ar2025~b7f898b33d.en.html) — European Central Bank*

**Albania's central bank data shows digital channels now dominate payment flows**

*Bank of Albania data reveals that digital channels have overtaken physical ones for payment transactions, with open banking services going live for the first time in early 2025 and work beginning on an instant payments system.*

The Albanian Daily News reports that the Bank of Albania's latest figures show digital payment channels now handle the majority of bank payment volumes – a threshold crossing for a market that has historically been cash-heavy. More notable for the European payments landscape is the operational detail: the first open banking transactions under Albania's transposition of PSD2 took place in early 2025, and the central bank has begun work on a domestic instant payments system.

Albania is not yet an EU member state, but it is an accession candidate actively aligning its payments regulation with the EU acquis. The sequencing is instructive. The country adopted PSD2's framework through its 2020 law on payment services and is now layering on open banking and instant payments infrastructure in parallel – compressing a timeline that took EU members the better part of a decade. For infrastructure vendors and payment networks active in south-east Europe, this represents a market in active build-out. For policymakers, it offers a case study in how accession candidates absorb EU payments regulation ahead of membership.

*Editorial note: A small market, but the story illustrates how EU payments regulation is shaping infrastructure development beyond the bloc's current borders – relevant context for firms with south-east European strategies.*

*Sources: [Digital Channels Dominate Bank Payments](https://albaniandailynews.com/news/digital-channels-dominate-bank-payments) — Albanian Daily News*

**New York proposes state-level open banking legislation**

*New York's proposed Financial Data Rights Act would create a state-level open banking regime for consumer and small business data, adding regulatory pressure independently of the CFPB's contested federal rulemaking.*

The Davis Wright Tremaine analysis, published May 4, outlines a proposed New York state law that would establish rights for consumers and small businesses to access and share their financial data through third-party providers. The bill creates a framework that parallels, and in some respects goes beyond, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Section 1033 rulemaking – which remains under political challenge at the federal level.

For European payments professionals, the significance is twofold. First, it signals that US open banking may develop through a patchwork of state laws rather than a single federal framework, creating a fragmented compliance environment for firms operating transatlantically. New York's weight in financial services means its rules would have outsized practical effect regardless of federal outcomes. Second, the bill covers small business data access, an area where the EU's own open finance agenda is still being shaped. How New York defines data holder obligations and liability allocation could influence thinking on both sides of the Atlantic.

*Editorial note: State-level open banking legislation in the US's largest financial centre has direct implications for European firms with US operations and for the broader trajectory of open finance regulation globally.*

*Sources: [New York Proposes Open Banking-Style Financial Data Access Legislation](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/financial-services-law-advisor/2026/05/ny-state-financial-data-access-legislation) — Davis Wright Tremaine*
