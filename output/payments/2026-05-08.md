**Lagarde sets out ECB framework for stablecoins and the future of money**

*The ECB president has drawn a sharp line between the functions money performs and the instruments that carry them, framing the policy case for the digital euro against private stablecoins.*

Christine Lagarde's speech today positions the ECB's thinking on stablecoins not as a technology question but as a monetary sovereignty question. By separating money's functions (unit of account, store of value, medium of exchange) from the instruments that deliver them, Lagarde argues that privately issued stablecoins can perform payment functions without providing the stability guarantees that central bank money underpins. The implication is clear: the ECB sees a role for stablecoins in payments but not as a substitute for sovereign money.

The speech arrives as the EU's MiCA framework beds in and the digital euro legislative process continues. For payments firms, the practical signal is that the ECB intends to remain the anchor of euro-denominated settlement, and that stablecoin issuers operating in Europe should expect their instruments to be treated as complements to, not competitors with, central bank infrastructure. The full text has been published on the ECB's website, though the detailed summary is not yet available.

*Editorial note: A speech by the ECB president directly framing the policy relationship between stablecoins and central bank money is material for any European payments strategist — it signals the intellectual framework that will shape regulation.*

**FCA opens competition probe into Visa, Mastercard and PayPal over digital wallet practices**

*The UK's financial regulator is investigating whether contractual arrangements between card schemes and digital wallet operators restrict competition, following a surge in wallet-based transactions.*

The FCA has launched a formal investigation under the Competition Act 1998 into the conduct of Visa, Mastercard and PayPal in the digital wallet market. The probe focuses on whether agreements governing how card payments are processed through digital wallets such as Apple Pay and Google Pay limit the ability of rival payment methods to compete at the point of sale. Digital wallet usage in the UK jumped from 8% to 29% of card transactions by 2023, a pace of adoption that has outstripped regulatory oversight.

The investigation builds on joint market reviews by the FCA and the Payment Systems Regulator that flagged concentration concerns. With approximately 95% of UK card transactions running through Visa and Mastercard rails, regulators are now asking whether digital wallets have entrenched that dominance rather than challenged it. For account-to-account payment providers and open banking firms, the probe could eventually create space for alternative payment methods within wallet interfaces, though any structural remedies are likely years away.

The timing is notable given Treasury's concurrent decision to fold the PSR into the FCA, consolidating payments competition oversight under a single authority.

*Editorial note: This is the most significant UK competition action in payments this year — it directly addresses the structural question of whether digital wallets reinforce card scheme dominance or could be opened to alternative rails.*

**Treasury confirms PSR functions will be merged into the FCA**

*HM Treasury will proceed with consolidating the Payment Systems Regulator into the Financial Conduct Authority, reshaping how UK payments are supervised.*

Following a public consultation, Treasury has confirmed its intention to fold the PSR's functions into the FCA. Norton Rose Fulbright's analysis, published May 7, outlines the response. The move ends years of debate about whether having two regulators with overlapping payments mandates created duplication or complementary oversight. Treasury's view is that a single regulator will provide clearer accountability and more coherent supervision across payments, consumer protection and competition.

For the payments industry, the merger means firms currently navigating separate regulatory relationships with the FCA and PSR will eventually deal with one body. The practical implications depend on implementation detail that is still to come, such as how the FCA will resource payments-specific competition work and whether the PSR's more interventionist posture on issues such as interchange and APP fraud reimbursement will survive the transition. The timing, alongside the FCA's new digital wallet probe, suggests the consolidated regulator intends to be active on payments competition from the outset.

*Editorial note: The confirmation that the PSR-FCA merger will proceed is a significant structural change to UK payments regulation, with direct implications for how firms are supervised and how competition policy is enforced.*

**Open Banking Limited appoints former FCA general counsel to board**

*OBL has added Sean Martin, previously general counsel at the FCA, as an independent non-executive director as the organisation navigates the transition toward open finance.*

Open Banking Limited announced on May 8 that Sean Martin has joined its board. Martin served as general counsel at the Financial Conduct Authority, giving him direct experience of the regulatory architecture that governs open banking and will shape the open finance framework. The appointment comes as the FCA's open finance roadmap and the government's Smart Data Strategy both signal an expansion of mandated data sharing beyond payments accounts.

Whether this is a new board seat or a replacement for a departing director is not stated in the available sources. What is clear is that OBL is adding regulatory expertise at a moment when its future role is under active discussion. The organisation's mandate was originally tied to the CMA's retail banking order; as open finance broadens the scope, OBL's governance and legitimacy need to keep pace with an expanding remit.

*Editorial note: The appointment signals OBL's need for regulatory credibility as it positions itself within the emerging open finance governance structure — the individual's FCA background is directly relevant to OBL's strategic challenges.*

**Currensea secures Dutch licence for continental European expansion**

*The UK open banking-based travel card provider has obtained a Dutch payments licence, establishing a base for cross-border operations under EU regulation.*

Currensea, which connects its debit card product directly to users' existing bank accounts via open banking, has secured regulatory approval in the Netherlands and established a local team to lead its European business. The Dutch licence gives Currensea passporting rights across the EU, allowing it to offer its services beyond the UK for the first time.

The move reflects a pattern familiar since Brexit: UK-based fintechs establishing EU-regulated entities to maintain and grow their European footprint. For Currensea specifically, the expansion tests whether its open banking-dependent model, which avoids prefunded wallets by initiating payments directly from customer bank accounts, translates to markets where open banking infrastructure varies in maturity. The appointment of Simone Aurighi as chief compliance and risk officer and management board member for the Dutch entity suggests the company is building local governance rather than running a shell operation.

*Editorial note: A practical example of a UK fintech navigating post-Brexit regulatory geography, and a test case for whether open banking-based payment models can scale across the EU's fragmented banking landscape.*
