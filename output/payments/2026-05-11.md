**UK banking-as-a-service entrant Keel pivots from neobank to infrastructure play**

*Keel's emergence from stealth as a BaaS platform, after pivoting away from its original neobank model, adds another contender to a segment that has seen both rapid growth and high-profile failures.*

Keel, a UK-based fintech, has launched a banking-as-a-service platform offering ledger infrastructure, open banking capabilities and access to domestic and international payment rails such as Faster Payments, Bacs, CHAPS, SEPA, SWIFT, ACH and Fedwire. The company pivoted from its original neobank concept to focus on providing infrastructure to other fintechs and non-bank businesses.

The timing is notable. European BaaS has been through a rough patch, with regulatory scrutiny of sponsor-bank models and the collapse of several intermediaries forcing the market to mature quickly. New entrants now need to demonstrate not just breadth of rail access but robust compliance architecture and sustainable unit economics. Keel's multi-rail, multi-currency positioning suggests an ambition to serve cross-border use cases, though the company will compete against established players such as ClearBank, Griffin and Modulr for UK-originated business. Whether the pivot from consumer-facing to infrastructure reflects a strategic insight or a retreat from a harder market remains to be seen.

*Editorial note: A new UK BaaS entrant is relevant to European payments professionals given ongoing consolidation in the segment and the regulatory questions that continue to shape the sponsor-bank model.*

**European banks begin exploring stablecoins for payments and treasury**

*A visible shift is under way as European banks move from watching stablecoin developments to actively evaluating them for settlement and liquidity management.*

European banks are examining stablecoins as tools for payments and treasury operations, according to IBS Intelligence. The interest centres on euro-denominated stablecoins that could sit alongside instant payments and traditional bank deposits rather than replace them, positioning these instruments within the regulated financial system rather than outside it.

This matters because MiCA has given Europe a regulatory framework that, for the first time, makes bank-issued or bank-distributed stablecoins a realistic proposition. The practical question is whether stablecoins offer genuine efficiency gains over SEPA Instant Credit Transfer for euro settlement, or whether their value lies primarily in programmability and interoperability with tokenised assets. For payments strategists, the signal is that stablecoins are no longer solely a crypto-native concern; they are entering the vocabulary of bank treasury teams and payment product leads. How quickly that translates into live products will depend on clearing infrastructure, interbank standards and, critically, customer demand.

*Editorial note: European banks actively exploring stablecoins for core functions represents a meaningful step beyond theoretical discussions, particularly under MiCA's regulatory clarity.*

**Visa selects seven fintechs from Greece, Cyprus and Malta for European innovation programme**

*Visa's latest European cohort focuses on open finance, data and embedded payments, extending the network's startup pipeline into south-east European markets that have been underrepresented in earlier rounds.*

Visa has chosen seven startups from Cyprus, Greece and Malta for the 2026 edition of its Visa Innovation Program Europe. The selected companies span thematic areas such as open finance, data services and embedded payments. The programme provides mentorship, Visa network access and potential commercial partnerships.

The geographic focus is the notable element here. South-east European markets are at an earlier stage of fintech maturity than western Europe, but they are also less encumbered by legacy infrastructure, which can make them fertile ground for new payment models. For Visa, extending the programme into these markets serves a dual purpose: identifying potential partners or acquisition targets early, and deepening relationships with local regulators and banks. The inclusion of open finance as an explicit thematic pillar reflects the direction of EU policy and Visa's broader strategy of positioning itself as infrastructure for data-driven financial services, not just card transactions.

*Editorial note: Visa's deliberate expansion of its innovation programme into smaller European markets signals where the network sees growth potential and how it is positioning around open finance.*

**Lloyds highlights open banking data as route to financial inclusion**

*Lloyds Banking Group's latest financial empowerment report advocates using open banking transaction data in credit scoring, a practical application that remains underdeployed across UK lending.*

Lloyds Banking Group has published a report arguing that incorporating alternative data sources, such as rent payments, utility bills and open banking transaction data, into credit scoring could unlock mainstream credit for underserved consumers. The report frames this as part of the bank's broader financial wellbeing strategy.

The argument is not new, but its source gives it weight. Lloyds is the UK's largest retail bank by current account share, and its public endorsement of open banking data for credit decisioning signals growing institutional willingness to move beyond compliance-driven API provision toward commercial use cases. The gap between advocacy and implementation remains wide: most UK lenders still rely predominantly on credit bureau data, and integrating real-time transaction data into automated decisioning requires changes to risk models, data governance and regulatory approval processes. For open banking advocates, having a major incumbent make this case publicly is useful; for the market, the question is whether Lloyds will embed this in its own lending processes or merely recommend it as an industry direction.

*Editorial note: A Tier 1 UK bank explicitly advocating open banking data for credit scoring is a meaningful data point for the commercialisation of open banking beyond payments.*

**SAMA licenses 32nd payments firm as Saudi open banking ecosystem expands**

*Saudi Arabia's central bank has licensed Tatbiq Darahem Company for Technology Almaliya to provide open banking services, bringing the total number of licensed payment services firms in the kingdom to 32.*

SAMA has granted an open banking licence to Tatbiq Darahem Company for Technology Almaliya, continuing a steady cadence of licensing that has built one of the larger open banking ecosystems in the Gulf region. With 32 licensed entities now operating, Saudi Arabia's payment services market is developing competitive density at a pace that outstrips several European markets.

For European payments professionals, the Saudi trajectory offers a useful comparison point. The kingdom is building its open banking framework in parallel with real-time payments infrastructure rather than retrofitting it onto an existing system, as Europe has done with PSD2 and is doing with PSD3/PSR. The volume of licensed firms also raises questions about sustainability: at some point, market consolidation is likely, and the dynamics of that process may offer lessons for other jurisdictions where licensing has been more restrictive. SAMA's approach of licensing generously and letting the market sort itself out contrasts with the UK's more cautious model and Europe's emerging framework under the Financial Data Access regulation.

*Editorial note: The pace of Saudi open banking licensing is relevant as a benchmark for European professionals tracking how different regulatory approaches shape market structure.*
