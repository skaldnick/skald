**UK King's Speech confirms merger of Payment Systems Regulator into FCA**

*The UK government has formally committed to folding the PSR into the Financial Conduct Authority, the most significant restructuring of UK payments oversight since the PSR's creation in 2015.*

The legislative agenda set out in the King's Speech on May 11 includes a Financial Services Bill that will merge the PSR into the FCA. The move, long trailed in policy circles, consolidates payments regulation under a single conduct authority and ends the PSR's decade as an independent body. Reports indicate the Bill will also reform the Financial Ombudsman Service.

For payments firms operating in the UK, the practical question is how the FCA will absorb the PSR's specific mandate on competition and access in payment systems. The PSR has been an assertive regulator on interchange, APP fraud liability and scheme access. Whether that agenda retains the same priority within a larger, more generalist body is the key uncertainty. Industry participants should expect a consultation period on the transition, but the legislative signal is now unambiguous.

*Editorial note: The PSR-FCA merger is the most consequential UK payments regulatory development in years, directly affecting how payment systems are supervised and how competition policy is enforced.*

**AMLA flags instant payments and AI-enabled fraud as growing AML weak spots**

*Europe's new anti-money laundering authority has identified instant payments, crypto-assets and AI-driven fraud as areas where compliance frameworks are failing to keep pace with risk.*

The Anti-Money Laundering Authority's latest risk assessment warns that the speed and irrevocability of instant payments create vulnerabilities that existing AML controls were not designed to handle. The report also highlights AI-enabled fraud techniques, such as synthetic identities and deepfake-assisted social engineering, as compounding the challenge. Crypto-asset risks and sanctions evasion feature prominently alongside these concerns.

The timing matters. With the EU's Instant Payments Regulation now requiring all euro-area payment service providers to offer instant credit transfers, the volume of transactions flowing through these rails is rising sharply. AMLA's assessment amounts to a signal that supervisory expectations on transaction monitoring and fraud prevention for instant payments will tighten. Firms that have treated instant payment AML controls as an extension of their batch-processing frameworks should take note.

*Editorial note: AMLA's explicit identification of instant payments as an AML risk area is a regulatory signal with direct operational implications as the Instant Payments Regulation takes full effect.*

**Saudi Arabia's fintech licensing expands as SAMA engages open banking firms**

*Saudi Arabia's central bank has grown the number of licensed finance and payment companies to 74, while holding direct discussions with open banking CEOs on the regulatory roadmap.*

Arab News reports that the Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) now licenses 32 payment providers, part of a broader total of 74 finance licences. Separately, Drahim has become the first Saudi app to receive an open banking licence from SAMA. The regulator also convened a meeting with open banking firm CEOs in Riyadh to discuss regulatory developments, signalling active engagement rather than passive licensing.

For European firms, the Saudi trajectory is worth watching. SAMA is building an open banking framework that borrows from European regulatory architecture but is moving with a pace and centralised coordination that the EU's fragmented approach has struggled to achieve. The direct dialogue between the regulator and licensed firms echoes the UK's early CMA-driven open banking model. As European open finance providers look for international expansion opportunities, Saudi Arabia's structured approach and growing licensed ecosystem make it one of the more substantive markets outside Europe.

*Editorial note: Saudi Arabia's open banking programme is maturing rapidly, and the combination of new licensing and direct regulatory engagement represents a meaningful data point for European firms tracking international open finance developments.*

**UK government signals commercial credit data sharing will sit alongside open finance**

*A government response on access-to-finance policy indicates that commercial credit data sharing requirements will be developed in tandem with the open finance framework, not as a separate track.*

The UK government has published its response to consultation on commercial credit data sharing (CCDS) and the bank referral scheme. Notably, it states that it will take further evidence on what categories of information to include and how to balance CCDS reporting with open finance obligations. This suggests the government views the two regimes as complementary rather than competing, and that open finance will play a structural role in SME lending infrastructure.

This is a practical signal for firms building in the open finance space. If commercial credit data flows are routed through open finance rails rather than bespoke reporting schemes, it creates a larger addressable market for API-based data providers and credit platforms. It also implies that the scope of the UK's open finance framework, when legislated, may be broader than personal finance data alone.

*Editorial note: The explicit linkage between commercial credit data policy and open finance is new and has implications for how the UK's open finance framework is scoped.*

**Banca Sella's Q1 profit rises 12%, driven by open finance platform growth**

*The Italian banking group's first-quarter results show open finance and digital platforms contributing materially to a double-digit rise in service revenue.*

Banca Sella reported quarterly profit above €112 million, with net interest income up 7.8% to €146.8 million and net income from services rising 11.8% to €132.8 million. Il Sole 24 Ore attributes part of the performance to the group's Hype digital banking platform and its broader open finance capabilities.

Banca Sella is one of the few mid-sized European banks where open finance is visibly flowing through to earnings. The service revenue growth is notable because it suggests monetisation of platform and API-based services beyond simple account provision. For an industry still searching for proof that open finance delivers revenue rather than just compliance cost, Sella's results offer a concrete, if institution-specific, data point.

*Editorial note: Earnings that demonstrably link open finance to revenue growth are rare in European banking; Banca Sella's results provide useful evidence for the commercial case.*
