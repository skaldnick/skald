**Azerbaijan sets open finance goals within 2027–2030 financial strategy**

*Azerbaijan has outlined plans to expand open banking into a broader open finance model as part of a four-year financial sector development strategy, adding to the growing list of non-EU countries adopting the framework.*

Azerbaijan's financial sector development strategy for 2027–2030 includes explicit goals for expanding open banking into open finance, covering a wider set of financial products and data-sharing arrangements. The announcement positions the country alongside a wave of jurisdictions outside the EU – such as the Philippines, which filed its own open finance legislation this month, and India, which continues to scale its Account Aggregator ecosystem – that are building regulatory frameworks for financial data portability.

For European firms, the significance is less about Azerbaijan's domestic market and more about the pattern. Open finance is becoming a global regulatory norm rather than a European export. As frameworks proliferate, cross-border interoperability questions will grow sharper, and firms operating across multiple jurisdictions face increasing compliance surface area. The EU's own open finance framework, still working through the legislative process, will need to account for a world where its rules are one of several competing models rather than the template.

**Australian mutual banks choose fintech partners over building open banking capabilities in-house**

*Australia's mutual banking sector is opting to partner with fintechs for open banking data recipient use cases rather than build internally, a pattern with relevance for smaller European institutions facing similar build-or-buy decisions.*

According to Banking Day, Australian mutual banks are favouring fintech partnerships where open banking data access "generates more customer value." The approach reflects a pragmatic calculation: for smaller institutions without the engineering capacity of major banks, partnering is faster, cheaper and carries less execution risk than building proprietary open banking infrastructure.

The dynamic mirrors what is playing out among smaller European banks and credit unions as PSD3 and the Financial Data Access regulation take shape. The question of whether to build, buy or partner for data-sharing capabilities is becoming urgent for institutions that lack the scale to justify large platform investments. Australia's Consumer Data Right regime, now several years into implementation, offers a useful reference point. The mutual banks' experience suggests that the ecosystem layer – specialist fintechs providing connectivity and use-case development – may be where most of the value accrues for smaller players, rather than in direct infrastructure ownership.

*Editorial note: Selected because the build-versus-partner question for open banking capabilities is directly relevant to smaller European banks preparing for FIDA, and Australia's more mature regime offers instructive signals.*

**API proliferation forces banks to rethink security architectures**

*The expansion of open banking, embedded finance and real-time payments has made APIs a primary attack surface for financial institutions, requiring security approaches that go beyond perimeter defence.*

PYMNTS reports that the rapid growth of API-based connectivity across financial services is driving a fundamental rethink of bank security strategies. Open banking frameworks, embedded finance partnerships and instant payment rails have collectively pushed API adoption well past the point where traditional perimeter-based security models are sufficient. Financial institutions are now dealing with a sprawling mesh of external connections, each representing a potential vulnerability.

The challenge is not abstract. As European banks prepare for the expanded data-sharing obligations under FIDA and the operational resilience requirements of DORA, API security is moving from a technology concern to a regulatory one. The volume of third-party connections will only increase, and the consequences of a breach – both financial and reputational – are growing. Institutions that treated API security as an extension of existing IT security are finding that the threat model is materially different: the attack surface is dynamic, distributed and largely outside their direct control.

*Editorial note: Selected because API security is becoming a board-level concern for European banks as data-sharing obligations expand, and this piece captures the operational reality well.*

**Verification of payee: practical implementation questions come into focus**

*As the EU's October deadline for verification of payee approaches, a Finextra analysis highlights the operational complexities that payment service providers still need to resolve.*

A detailed Finextra piece by Banfico's CEO examines what payment service providers need to consider before implementing verification of payee, the name-checking requirement designed to reduce misdirected payments and authorised push payment fraud. The EU's Instant Payments Regulation mandates VoP for euro credit transfers by October, and the piece suggests that many providers are still working through fundamental implementation questions such as data matching tolerances, handling of non-Latin character sets and integration with existing payment workflows.

The UK's Confirmation of Payee scheme, now several years old, offers lessons but is not a direct blueprint – the EU's scheme operates across multiple languages, character sets and domestic payment infrastructures, adding layers of complexity. For payment processors and banks, the risk is not just missing the deadline but implementing VoP in a way that generates excessive false positives, degrading the customer experience without meaningfully reducing fraud. The balance between matching strictness and usability is a design problem that each provider will need to calibrate, and there is limited time to get it right.

*Editorial note: Selected because the EU VoP deadline is approaching and implementation challenges are material and under-discussed relative to the policy debate.*
