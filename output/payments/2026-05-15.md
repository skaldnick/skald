**STET chief says SEPA will underpin European payments sovereignty**

*The head of France's clearing infrastructure argues that instant payments regulation and domestic card schemes are converging to reduce European dependence on global networks.*

Régis Folbaum, CEO of STET, told FinextraTV at Diebold Nixdorf's Intersect event in Cannes that SEPA is positioned to become the backbone of a new phase of European payments independence. He pointed to growing adoption of domestic card schemes alongside the instant payments regulation as evidence that alignment across the eurozone is accelerating and creating an alternative to reliance on US-headquartered networks.

Folbaum was notably candid about the fintech-bank relationship, describing fintechs as carrying "a certain arrogance" while banks default to mergers and acquisitions rather than building. The comments come as the European Commission continues to push for a unified European payments strategy, with the instant payments regulation now in force and the digital euro still under legislative debate. STET, which processes the bulk of French retail payments and operates across several European markets, has a direct commercial interest in SEPA's centrality, but Folbaum's framing reflects a broader industry conversation about strategic autonomy that is gaining political traction.

*Editorial note: A senior infrastructure operator articulating the sovereignty argument publicly, and commenting frankly on fintech-bank dynamics, advances a continuing European policy debate in a way that matters to readers positioning for SEPA's next phase.*

**Adyen partners with SAP to embed payments into enterprise resource planning**

*The tie-up signals that payment orchestration is moving deeper into back-office systems, not just checkout experiences.*

Adyen and SAP have announced a collaboration to launch what SAP is calling its Unified Payment solution, integrating Adyen's payment processing directly into SAP's enterprise software stack. The deal gives SAP's large corporate customer base access to Adyen's acquiring and payment capabilities without requiring separate integrations or middleware.

For Adyen, this extends its reach further into enterprise and B2B commerce, a segment where payment complexity is high but adoption of modern payment orchestration has lagged consumer-facing channels. For SAP, it fills a gap: enterprise resource planning systems have historically treated payments as an afterthought, handled by treasury or finance teams through separate banking portals. Embedding payments natively into ERP workflows is a logical step as businesses expect real-time reconciliation and consolidated reporting. The partnership is worth watching less for the two companies involved and more for what it confirms about where payment infrastructure is headed: away from the point of sale and into the operational core of large organisations.

*Editorial note: This goes beyond a vendor announcement — it illustrates the structural shift of payments processing into enterprise back-office systems, a trend with implications for banks, processors and treasury teams across Europe.*

**South African fintech Stitch raises $25 million as African open finance scales**

*Andreessen Horowitz leads the Series A for a payments and data platform operating across South Africa, Nigeria and other African markets, underlining investor confidence in open finance infrastructure outside Europe.*

Stitch, which provides account-to-account payment initiation and financial data access across several African markets, has closed a $25 million Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz. The round is one of the larger recent raises for an African open finance infrastructure provider and reflects continued venture appetite for payment rails in markets where card penetration remains low and real-time account-based payments are the primary digital channel.

The raise matters for European observers for two reasons. First, Stitch's model, connecting merchants and platforms directly to bank accounts via API, mirrors the pay-by-bank infrastructure being built across Europe under PSD2 and its successors. Second, a16z's involvement suggests that major global investors see open finance infrastructure in emerging markets as a category with scale potential comparable to what has developed in Europe and Brazil. As European fintechs such as Banked are being acquired by banks looking to own this capability (NAB's deal last week being the most recent example), Stitch's fundraise is a data point on how the market values independent open finance platforms elsewhere.

*Editorial note: Relevant as a comparative signal — the same open finance infrastructure model being built in Europe is attracting major venture backing in Africa, and the parallels to European pay-by-bank are instructive.*

**UAE crypto brokerage Rain launches pay-by-bank under open finance framework**

*Rain is the first crypto brokerage in the UAE to offer bank-funded purchases via open banking rails, powered by Lean Technologies under the CBUAE's open finance regime.*

Rain, a regulated crypto brokerage operating in the UAE, has launched pay-by-bank functionality for its customers, allowing them to fund crypto purchases directly from their bank accounts rather than via cards or manual transfers. The service runs on infrastructure from Lean Technologies, which holds an open finance licence from the Central Bank of the UAE.

The move is notable less for crypto specifically and more for what it reveals about the UAE's open finance framework in practice. The CBUAE has been building out its regulatory infrastructure for open banking and open finance at a pace that puts it ahead of most Gulf peers. Rain's launch demonstrates that the framework is now operational enough to support live, consumer-facing use cases, and that open banking rails are being adopted beyond traditional payments and lending into adjacent financial services. For European firms watching Gulf markets as potential expansion territories or competitive benchmarks, the speed of deployment is worth noting: the UAE framework is producing commercial applications while the EU's own open finance regulation (FiDA) remains in legislative negotiation.

*Editorial note: The UAE's open finance framework producing a live consumer use case before FiDA is even finalised offers a useful comparative benchmark for European readers tracking global open finance progress.*
