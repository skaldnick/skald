**CFIT publishes first roadmap to bring open data principles to UK property**

*The Centre for Finance, Innovation and Technology has set out a plan to apply Smart Data principles to the homebuying process, marking the most concrete extension of the UK's open data programme beyond financial services.*

CFIT's Open Property Roadmap, published this week, outlines how consent-driven, interoperable data sharing could reduce the friction and opacity that characterise UK property transactions. The approach draws directly on the architecture established by open banking and the broader Smart Data framework, applying standardised APIs and permissioned access to property-related data such as title information, local authority searches and mortgage affordability.

The roadmap is significant less for its immediate practical effect — implementation will require coordination across conveyancers, lenders, local authorities and land registries — than for what it signals about the trajectory of the UK's Smart Data agenda. With the Financial Services Bill set to legislate open finance and the government already consulting on Smart Data in other sectors such as energy and telecoms, property represents the next domain where the open banking model is being tested as a template.

For payments and open banking professionals, the CFIT roadmap matters because it validates the portability of the underlying model. If consent-driven data sharing can work in a sector as fragmented and analogue as property, it strengthens the case for its application elsewhere.

**Lean Technologies scales pay-by-bank as UAE's open finance framework goes live**

*The UAE's open finance regulation has moved from framework to operation, with Lean Technologies reporting that its account-to-account payment volumes are growing under the new regime.*

Lean Technologies, a pay-by-bank provider operating in the Gulf, says it is scaling its account-to-account payments suite following the Abu Dhabi Financial Services Authority's activation of its open finance framework. The company claims its infrastructure has helped merchants avoid over $100 million in card fees, positioning direct bank payments as a cost-effective alternative in a market that has historically been card-dominant.

For European observers, the UAE's approach is worth watching as a real-time case study in how open finance regulation translates into payment volume. The ADFSA framework shares design principles with PSD2 and the forthcoming PSD3 — mandatory API access, consent-based data sharing, third-party provider licensing — but is being implemented in a market with different competitive dynamics and a smaller, more concentrated banking sector. How quickly account-to-account payments gain traction against cards in the UAE will offer early evidence on a question European regulators and providers are also grappling with.

*Editorial note: While not European, the UAE's open finance framework going live is one of the first implementations outside Europe and the UK to draw heavily on PSD2-style design. Its progress is a relevant comparator for this audience.*

**European issuers view digital euro as part of a coexistence model, not a standalone rail**

*FIS says European card issuers are not evaluating the digital euro in isolation but as one element of a broader ecosystem alongside stablecoins and tokenised assets.*

Speaking at NextGen Nordics 2026 in Stockholm, Francesco Dallesandro, head of sales for cards and payments in Europe at FIS, said issuers are thinking about central bank digital currencies, regulated stablecoins and tokenised assets as a coexisting set of instruments rather than converging towards a single digital payment rail. He suggested that end customers care about experience and fraud reduction, not about the specific mechanics of which digital asset underpins a transaction.

The framing matters because much of the European debate around the digital euro has treated it as a distinct policy initiative, separate from the private-sector stablecoin market and the tokenisation agenda. If issuers are indeed approaching these as a single ecosystem to be managed holistically, it has implications for how they allocate technology investment and how they engage with the ECB's digital euro project. It also suggests that the competitive dynamics between public and private digital money may be less binary than the policy debate sometimes implies.

*Editorial note: This is a conference panel rather than hard news, but the substance — how issuers are actually framing the digital euro relative to private digital assets — is a useful signal for European payments strategists.*
