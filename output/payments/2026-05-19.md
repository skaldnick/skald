**Mastercard positions AI-powered intelligence as key to account-to-account payment adoption**

*Mastercard is framing open finance data as the solution to merchant friction in account-to-account payments, signalling how card networks intend to stay relevant as payment initiation shifts away from cards.*

Mastercard has published a detailed articulation of how its expanding account-to-account payment intelligence offering uses open finance data to address what it identifies as the core merchant pain points in non-card payments: failed transactions, lack of confirmation and limited retry logic. The company argues that by layering AI-driven analytics over open finance connections, it can give merchants the transaction visibility and success rates they currently get from card rails.

The strategic significance is clear. As instant payment infrastructure matures across Europe and regulators push account-to-account payments into the mainstream, the card networks face a disintermediation risk. Mastercard's response is not to resist the shift but to insert itself as an intelligence and orchestration layer on top of it. This mirrors Visa's own moves into account-to-account services and confirms that the major networks see their future role as data and routing providers, not solely as card scheme operators.

For merchants and payment service providers evaluating account-to-account adoption, the proposition is worth scrutinising: Mastercard is effectively arguing that open finance rails need card-network-grade tooling to work at scale, a framing that conveniently preserves its role in the value chain.

*Editorial note: This is a significant strategic signal from a major card network on how it plans to compete in an account-to-account payments world, directly relevant to European open finance and instant payment debates.*

**UK gambling industry mounts legal challenge over open banking affordability checks**

*The UK betting sector's legal pushback against open banking-based affordability checks highlights the tension between consumer protection regulation and commercial resistance to data-sharing mandates.*

UK gambling operators are preparing a legal challenge against the Gambling Commission's forthcoming affordability check regime, which would require operators to use open banking data to verify customers' financial capacity to gamble. The industry body argues the checks are disproportionate and will drive customers toward unregulated operators. The challenge is expected to test the legal limits of regulators mandating open banking data use in sectors beyond financial services.

This matters for the open banking sector because gambling is one of the first non-financial-services use cases where open banking data sharing has moved from voluntary adoption to regulatory compulsion. If the legal challenge succeeds, it could slow the extension of open banking-style data mandates into adjacent sectors. If it fails, it sets a precedent for regulators in other areas, such as rental affordability or social lending, to require open banking verification.

The case also exposes a practical tension: open banking's value proposition depends partly on consumer consent and trust, yet regulatory mandates effectively bypass consumer choice by making checks a condition of service. How this is resolved will shape the broader trajectory of smart data adoption in the UK.

*Editorial note: This story crystallises a live tension between open banking's expanding regulatory footprint and commercial resistance, with implications for how data-sharing mandates are applied beyond core financial services.*

**Nationwide introduces in-app controls to block abusive payment reference messages**

*Nationwide's new feature allowing abuse survivors to filter harmful content from payment references is a practical response to a growing recognition that payment systems can be weaponised for coercive control.*

Nationwide has launched an in-app tool that lets customers block payment messages from specific senders, directly addressing the use of bank transfer reference fields as a channel for harassment and coercive control by abusers. The feature gives survivors control over which payment messages they see, without blocking the payments themselves.

The move reflects a broadening understanding across UK banking that payment infrastructure carries risks beyond fraud. Domestic abuse charities have long flagged that payment reference fields, which are unfiltered free-text, are routinely exploited to send threatening or controlling messages to victims. Nationwide is the first major UK financial institution to offer a customer-facing control for this specific problem.

For the industry, this raises a question about whether such protections should become standard. The Payments Systems Regulator and FCA have both highlighted economic abuse as a priority. If Nationwide's approach proves effective, pressure will build on other banks and building societies to offer similar functionality, potentially through Pay.UK's messaging standards or as a feature expectation under the Consumer Duty.

*Editorial note: A concrete product response to an under-addressed harm in payment systems, with potential to influence industry standards and regulatory expectations.*

**Zepz joins NCA partnership targeting financial signals of child sexual abuse**

*The remittance group behind WorldRemit has joined a UK law enforcement public-private partnership focused on identifying payment patterns linked to child sexual exploitation, extending financial crime intelligence efforts into the money transfer sector.*

Zepz, parent company of WorldRemit and Sendwave, has announced its participation in a National Economic Crime Centre cell specifically targeting child sexual abuse. The partnership brings remittance operators into a collaborative intelligence-sharing framework previously dominated by banks, with the aim of identifying financial transaction patterns that indicate exploitation.

The significance for the payments sector is twofold. First, it extends the financial crime detection perimeter beyond traditional banking into cross-border remittance flows, where regulatory oversight has historically been lighter. Second, it reflects the NCA's evolving approach of building sector-specific cells rather than relying on broad-brush suspicious activity reporting. For payment service providers, participation in such partnerships is increasingly becoming a marker of regulatory maturity and a practical expectation rather than a voluntary initiative.

*Editorial note: Remittance providers joining targeted financial crime intelligence efforts marks a meaningful expansion of the payments industry's role in law enforcement collaboration.*
